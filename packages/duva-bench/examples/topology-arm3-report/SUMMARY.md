## Result

| Arm | n | Total cost | Avg cost/trial | Avg tokens in | Avg tokens out | Avg tool calls | Avg wall clock |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `serial` (single agent) | 10 | $4.46 | $0.45 | 358,988 | 17,466 | 16.5 | 115.1s |
| `fanout` (swarm: planner + implementer + reviewer) | 10 | $16.22 | $1.62 | 1,319,457 | 60,512 | 46.6 | 409.3s |

Acceptance score, the pre-registered primary metric, did **not** move with topology:
`serial` 0.97–0.99 mean, `fanout` 0.97–0.97 mean across the two tasks — within this
study's own noise floor (sd 0.036, `report.json` → `noiseFloors`). Every other axis
(`api-shape`, `edge-cases`, `self-tests`) shows the same pattern: no topology effect
distinguishable from noise.

**What this shows, on these two toy tasks and this model:** the swarm topology costs
roughly **3.6x** the tokens and wall clock, **2.8x** the tool calls, of a single agent
on the same work, for no measurable quality difference. That is the finding A16 asked
for — cost and outcome of K-parallel-candidate fan-out vs one serial session — and it
points one way: fan-out bought nothing on tasks this small. It does not generalize past
`retry` and `semver`; a task where a single agent plausibly fails outright (needing a
second attempt from a different angle) is the case fan-out is supposed to help, and
neither example task here is hard enough to produce that case.

## What this does not show

- **Not a claim about fan-out in general.** `retry` and `semver` are both solvable in
  one pass by a single competent agent; this is close to the best case for `serial`
  and a correspondingly weak case for `fanout` to prove its worth. A task suite with a
  higher single-agent failure rate is the fairer test of D1's actual thesis.
- **One model, one provider, fast tier, five reps.** Calibrated against this track's
  prior runs (a 24-trial pilot at $8.03, ≈$0.33/trial), but the swarm arm's real
  per-trial cost (up to $1.82 on `semver`) ran well past that flat estimate — the
  average is right, the ceiling isn't. See the budget note below.
- **`retry` and `semver` are single-turn-per-agent tasks** (this track's ceiling, PLAN.md
  §3.2); a swarm's real advantage — recovering from a failed attempt via a second
  agent's fresh context — cannot show up under a brief built for one competent pass.

## Budget note (for the record)

This study's approved cap was $15. The scheduler's own budget gate (`scheduler.ts`
`takeNext()`) checks accumulated spend *within one process invocation* before starting
each trial, at concurrency 3 — so up to 2 trials already in flight when the threshold
crosses are not stopped, and per-trial cost on the `fanout`×`semver` cell ranged
$0.16–$7.20 (a 45x spread), far wider than the flat $0.40/trial planning estimate
anticipated. Actual spend reached $19.94 before the gate's own `budget-stop` fired
(`progress.jsonl`, `kind: "budget-stop"`, `spentUsd: 17.60` at the point it started
refusing new trials — two trials were already running past that point). The remaining
2 trials (`serial`×`semver`×r4, r5) were completed in a follow-up invocation once
that was noticed, for a final total of **$20.68** (see `costLedger` in `report.json`).
Reported to the study's author as a stop-and-report per the run's ground rules;
continuing to complete the study was the author's explicit call. This is worth a
finding for duva-bench itself, filed separately from ADP's M3: the gate should track
cumulative spend across resumed invocations and reserve budget for in-flight trials,
not just the invocation's own running total.
